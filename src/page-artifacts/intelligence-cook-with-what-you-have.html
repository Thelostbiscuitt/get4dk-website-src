01 / Decision rule

How do I decide what to cook from the ingredients I already have?

Pick 3–5 candidate meals, then eliminate anything that needs too many missing ingredients, too much time or ignores food that needs using.

That turns an open-ended inspiration problem into a constrained decision.

Step 01

Start with anchors.

Protein, starch, dominant vegetable or sauce base. These define the useful search space faster than listing every spice in the cupboard.

Step 02

Check the gap.

Ask what each option still requires. One missing onion is different from an entire second shopping list.

Step 03

Let urgency vote.

If spinach, tomatoes or cooked chicken need attention first, they should influence the ranking.

02 / Pantry coverage

What makes one recipe a better pantry match than another?

A strong match uses more of your meaningful ingredients, needs fewer new purchases and fits the time and equipment you actually have.

High coverage
Most structural ingredients already exist. Best starting point
Low shopping delta
Only one or two inexpensive additions materially improve the meal.
Freshness fit
The meal uses ingredients that need attention instead of letting novelty outrank waste.
Personal fit
Diet, dislikes, household needs and recent meals still matter after ingredient matching.
03 / Information gain

Why is this more useful than a generic recipe search?

Because “contains chicken” is not enough. The useful answer explains why an option fits your kitchen and what trade-off you are making.

Information gain / 4DK angle

4DK can turn pantry coverage, freshness, shopping delta, household context and recent meals into ranking signals. The public page teaches the decision model; the product can apply it to the user's actual kitchen.
